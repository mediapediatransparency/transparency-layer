Method

How it works

Every number on this site is counted by a computer program from Wikipedia's public records. The program does no guessing and uses no AI. Run it again on the same article and you get exactly the same numbers.

The stepsWhat each number meansWords used on this siteHow the work is checkedRulesLimits

The steps

  1. Take one exact version of the article. Wikipedia keeps every version of every article. Each of our pages names the version it describes and its date, with a link, so you can open exactly the same text.
  2. Find out who wrote each word. A free tool called WikiWho, run on Wikipedia's own servers, follows every word through the article's whole history and finds the editor who first typed it. If a word was deleted and later put back, it still belongs to the person who first wrote it. Moving a paragraph does not make you its author.
  3. Count only the text people read. Footnotes, tables, picture captions and lists of links are left out.
  4. Check each editor against Wikipedia's sanctions. We keep a hand-checked list of editors whom Wikipedia's administrators banned from a topic, banned from the site, or caught running fake extra accounts. Each entry links to Wikipedia's own decision.
  5. Follow every footnote to its source. Each footnote is matched to the newspaper, website, publisher, journal or organisation it points to. Books are matched by their ISBN (the number printed on the back of a book), using Open Library and Wikidata. Then we show what outside organisations say about each source. We add no rating of our own.
  6. Compare like with like. Each number is shown next to the figure for a typical similar article (people next to people, newspapers next to newspapers), so you can tell whether it is unusual.

What each number means

The numbers on an article page, in the order they appear. Each one says what was counted. None of them says whether the article is right or wrong.

Who wrote the text

  • Share of the words. Out of every 100 words in today's article, how many each editor first typed. “Editor 1” wrote the most; the numbers are not their real names and change from article to article.
  • Editors who wrote half the text. Line the editors up from most words to fewest and count how many it takes to reach half the article. A small number means a few people wrote most of it.
  • The opening section. The same figures for the summary at the top of the article (Wikipedia calls it the lead). It is the part most readers, search engines and AI assistants see.
  • When the text was added. The year each editor's surviving words were first typed.
  • How long new text lasts. Of all the words ever added to the article, including ones later deleted, how many were still there 2 days, 30 days and a year later.
  • Wikipedia's voice in the opening. Wikipedia's neutrality policy says contested assertions and opinions should not be stated as facts in Wikipedia's own voice but attributed to who holds them, and that uncontested facts should be stated plainly. For each sentence of the opening section we record two things. First, evidence it is contested: editors took it out for a day or more and put it back at least twice (by two or more accounts; removals undone within a day are mostly vandalism and are not counted), tagged it as disputed, or quoted it in three or more talk-page discussions or in a request for comment; or it uses a word from Wikipedia's own “Words to watch” guideline (labels, editorializing, unsupported attributions, expressions of doubt). Second, whether it names who holds the view: an author it cites, or a phrase such as “according to”, “historians argue”, “opponents characterize”, or “widely seen as” (the policy allows widespread views to be described as such), or “he”, “she” or “they” followed by a verb such as “argued”, “said” or “believed” (in an opening section these refer to a person or group already named). In that last case only the words after the phrase count as theirs: in “He notably argued …”, “notably” is still Wikipedia's own word. A sentence with the first and not the second is marked as one to check. That is not a finding that it breaks the policy: whether reliable sources really conflict is for readers and editors to judge. On control articles such as Photosynthesis no opening sentence is marked. “With attribution” puts the cited authors' surnames in front of the sentence by a fixed template (a leading word such as “However,” stays first); it is not a proposed edit, and nothing on this site is written by AI. The last column shows the most similar sentence of an earlier opening (1 January 2015, 2019 or 2023; content words in common, similarity at least 0.3) that cited works today's opening no longer cites, with those works and their authors named in front by the same template. The policy text quoted on every page is checked word for word against the policy page at the version linked.
  • Short description changes. The short description is the line Wikipedia shows under an article's title in its search box and apps. Each article's history from 1 January 2017 is checked at 128 evenly spaced edits, plus every edit whose summary mentions the short description and every edit Wikipedia tagged as later reverted. Wherever two checks differ, the edits between them are checked until the exact edit is found. Only the opening section is read. A change undone between two checks can still be missed. Changes put back to the earlier wording within a day are shown separately, because that is how vandalism and its repair usually look. Before about 2018 most short descriptions came from Wikidata, not the article, and are not tracked. Edit summaries are used only to choose which edits to check and are not kept; no account names are kept. Words that would disclose a person are replaced by a redacted tag naming the reason: links to an editor's page and IP addresses always, and personal details of private people that vandals wrote in, passage by passage (listed in data/redactions.json). The rest of the wording is shown as it was; Wikipedia's page history keeps the original.
  • The two template columns beside the opening. Both are today's first paragraph rewritten by fixed rules; neither is written by a person or by AI, and neither is a proposed edit. “With sources named”: each sentence marked by the check above gets its cited authors' surnames in front (“According to A, B and C, …”; more than three become “and N others”); every other sentence is left as it is. When a marked sentence's footnotes name no author, the sources' names go in front instead (“According to The New York Times, …”); when no footnote follows it, “According to an unspecified source, …”. “With removed sources kept”: the same, plus every work cited in the opening on 1 January 2015, 2019 or 2023 and not cited there now. The sentence it backed then goes back in, after today's most similar sentence (content words in common, similarity at least 0.3), or at the end when nothing today is similar and that sentence was in that date's first paragraph. It gets its own authors in front (or its sources' names, or “an unspecified source”, as above), unless it already names one of them or says who holds the view. An earlier sentence that still reads nearly the same today (similarity at least 0.85) is not repeated; its authors join the names in front of today's sentence when that sentence has names in front. Earlier sentences nearly the same as each other are shown once; scraps of infobox or markup are left out.
  • At a glance. At the top of each article page, the main figures drawn as bars: who wrote the words, the share by sanctioned editors, the kinds of sources cited, how researchers grade the cited publishers and journals, how easy the footnotes are to check, and who shares the cited websites. They are the same numbers as in the sections below.
  • How the opening has changed. The whole first paragraph as it read on 1 January 2015, 2019 and 2023 (those the article existed for) and today, side by side. Play on a version shows the one before it changing into it: words taken out fade in red, words added appear in green (compared word by word with Python's difflib). Below: how many of the earliest paragraph's words are still there in the same order, how many were taken out and added, and the share of meaningful words the two have in common (we leave out words like “the” and “of”). Above each version, the page's short description, the line Wikipedia shows under the title in its search box and apps (until about 2018 it came from Wikidata and is not in the article's text, so older versions often have none). Then the sources cited in footnotes inside the first paragraph at each date, with the same ratings as above. Folded below, the three opening sentences that changed most: each of today's opening sentences is paired with the old sentence that shares most of its meaningful words; pairs sharing between 30% and 90% are the same sentence, rewritten, and the three that share least are shown. The rules are the same for every article.
  • The sources cited then and now (under Sources cited). The whole article's footnotes on those same dates, with the same ratings as above. The ratings are today's, because past ratings are not on record: a change shows that different sources were cited, not that a source was rated differently. Below it, the sources are split into those dropped since the earliest date, those kept, and those added, each with the same ratings.
  • Word tracker and the words that changed most. On the search page, for any article: how often words appear per 1,000 words, twice a year. The six words that changed most (three up, three down) are picked by a standard statistical test that allows for how common a word is, so a jump from 20 to 60 mentions counts for more than one from 1 to 3. Words like “the” and “which” are left out.
  • How much older text is still there. Take the article as it was 1, 2, 3 and 5 years before the version we analysed, and count how many of its words are still in it. If only 17 out of every 100 words from three years ago are left, most of the article has been rewritten since then. Every article uses the same distances, so we never pick a date for one subject.

Editors Wikipedia sanctioned

  • Text by sanctioned editors. Out of every 100 words, how many were written by editors whom Wikipedia later sanctioned: banned from the topic (a topic ban), banned from Wikipedia (a site ban), or found running secret extra accounts (sockpuppets). A sanction is about how someone behaved on Wikipedia, not proof that what they wrote is wrong.
  • Before, during or after the sanction. Whether each of those words was added before the sanction, while it was in force, or after it ended.

Sources

  • Citations. A citation is a footnote: the little number that points to a book, article or website. A source is who published it. One source can be cited many times.
  • Works per sentence. One footnote can hold a list of several books or articles. We count every work in the footnotes that follow a sentence. A sentence backed by 17 works has more support listed than most; it does not mean the works all say the same thing.
  • Most-cited authors. The people named as authors in the footnotes, counted once for every footnote that names them. Where a footnote links an author to their Wikipedia article, we show what Wikidata records about that person: job, field, employer, party membership, offices held and organisations they belong to. A † means Wikidata gives no source for that fact. We never give an author a political score, and we do not look up Wikipedia editors at all.
  • Kind of source. Book publisher, university press, academic journal, newspaper and so on, as recorded in Wikidata, the free database that sits behind Wikipedia.
  • Wikipedia's own rating. Wikipedia's editors keep a list of about 530 sources they argue about often, marked “generally reliable”, “no consensus”, “generally unreliable” and so on. Most sources are not on the list; that is not a bad mark.
  • Quality score. From researchers (Lin and colleagues, 2023) who combined six sets of expert ratings of news websites into one score from 0 (lowest) to 1 (highest). Books and journals have no score.
  • Who shares a website (audience leaning). Researchers matched Twitter users to US voter records and counted how many Democrats and how many Republicans shared links to each website, treating both parties as the same size. The score runs from −1 (shared only by Democrats) through 0 (shared equally) to +1 (shared only by Republicans). To turn it into people: out of every 100 people sharing the site, Republicans = (1 + score) ÷ 2 × 100. So −0.4 means about 70 Democrats and 30 Republicans. It describes who shares a website, not what the website says, and covers only websites. Two studies are used: DomainDemo (Yang and colleagues, 2025) and an older one from 2017 Twitter data (Robertson and colleagues, 2018).
    Cited: Yang, K.-C., Goel, P., Quintana-Mathé, A., Horgan, L., McCabe, S. D., Grinberg, N., Joseph, K. & Lazer, D. (2025). DomainDemo: a dataset of domain-sharing activities among different demographic groups on Twitter. Scientific Data 12, 1251. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-025-05604-6 (derived party-leaning scores; the data repository states no licence). Robertson, R. E., Jiang, S., Joseph, K., Friedland, L., Lazer, D. & Wilson, C. (2018). Auditing partisan audience bias within Google Search. Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact. 2(CSCW), 148. https://doi.org/10.1145/3274417.
  • Media Bias/Fact Check and AllSides. Two organisations that label news outlets (left, centre, right, and how factual). Their labels are shown as they give them.
  • Academic level. Finland's Publication Forum, where panels of university researchers grade publishers and journals: 3 top, 2 leading, 1 basic scholarly, 0 below the scholarly bar. Levels from the Publication forum (julkaisufoorumi.fi), licensed CC BY 4.0; changes: this site matches them to the publishers and journals an article cites and counts citations by level.
  • Recorded left–right position. Where a source's recorded labels put it, from far left to far right, out of every 100 citations that can be placed. Labels that are positions (far-left, left-wing, centre-left, centrist, centre-right, right-wing, far-right) are used as they are. For a source with none of those, other labels are placed only when English Wikipedia's own article on that ideology puts it on one side as a whole: “conservative” on the right, “socialist” on the left, “social democratic” centre-left, and so on. “Liberal” is placed by country, because it means different things in different places: centre-left in the United States, Japan and South Korea, centre-right in Australia, Sweden and France, and not placed where Wikipedia says both sides use it (Britain, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Russia, Israel and others). Labels such as “Zionist”, “Islamist” and “nationalist” are not placed, because Wikipedia's articles describe them on more than one side. The full list, with Wikipedia's words for each. Only a small share of citations can be placed, so the page always says how many citations and how many sources the figure rests on.
  • Recorded as Zionist / anti-Zionist, Islamist, nationalist. These labels are not left or right, so they are counted on their own: the share of an article's citations to sources recorded with the label, and how many sources that is. Zionist and anti-Zionist are shown side by side. Each row appears on any article that cites at least one source with that label, so we never choose which articles get it.
  • Recorded political affiliation. What Wikidata and Wikipedia's own category pages say about a publisher's politics (for example “left-wing” or “conservative”). “None recorded” means there is a record but it says nothing about politics; “no entry” means there is no record at all.
  • Sources its writers cite. The same ratings for every footnote this article's editors added across all the articles we analysed, with each editor counted in proportion to how much of this article they wrote. No single editor's figure is ever shown.
  • Harder-to-check footnotes. Footnotes with no web link, books with no page number, sources behind a paywall, opinion pieces, sources not in English with no quote or translation, and dead links with no saved copy. Each makes a footnote harder to check, not wrong.
  • Links to check. Cited websites whose home page our program could not open, and websites whose address (domain) was registered to someone after the article cited it, which can mean a new owner. We show the site's title then and now so you can compare.

How the article was argued over

  • Text removed. How many words editors deleted over the article's history, and how many of those were later put back.
  • Words to watch and contested terms. Words from Wikipedia's own guide to loaded wording (such as “terrorist”, “claimed”, “legendary”), and groups of rival words for the same thing, counted when editors added or removed them. A count says a word was added or removed, not whether that was right.
  • How often it changes. Edits per year, edits and editors in the last year, and how many edits came from people without an account.
  • Back-and-forth. Words that were taken out and put back at least twice, each time staying out for a day or more. Words removed and restored within a day are counted separately, because that is how vandalism and its quick repair usually look.
  • Sentences taken out and put back. A sentence is “taken out” when one edit deletes at least half of its words, and “put back” when a later edit restores them. We count how many times that happened, how many different accounts did it (never who), and since when the sentence has stayed. Edits that wipe most of the page at once are left out, because that is almost always vandalism.
  • Rules that kept text in place. Records showing that Wikipedia's own processes stopped text being changed for a time: a discussion ban (editors agreed not to reopen a question until a set date), a request for comment (a formal vote-like discussion whose result is hard to undo), edit requests turned down (on locked pages, editors without enough experience must ask; we count requests that quote the sentence, and those closed with a ban mentioned), a hidden note in the page's source asking editors to discuss before changing a sentence, a disputed tag, and page protection. We read these from the talk page and its archives as they were on the date of the version, and match a discussion to a sentence when it quotes eight or more words of it in a row.
  • Protection. When Wikipedia locked the article so that only some editors could change it, and at what level.

Comparisons

  • Typical. The middle value (the median) for the other analysed articles of the same kind: half of them are above it, half below. Shown first for the same topic area, then for all areas.

ISBN

A free database run alongside Wikipedia that records facts about things, such as who owns a newspaper.

The discussion page behind every Wikipedia article, where editors argue about changes and place notices.
Pattern ID
A code like 4F44YQ that stands for one editor across articles, so patterns can be seen without naming anyone.
Request for comment (RfC)
A formal discussion that invites editors from all over Wikipedia to settle a question about an article. Someone not involved closes it and writes down the result.
Discussion ban (moratorium)
An agreement that nobody will propose changing a passage again until a set date, to stop the same argument starting over.
Edit request
On a locked page, editors who are not allowed to edit it ask on the talk page for a change. Another editor accepts or declines it.
Page protection
A lock an administrator puts on a page. “Extended confirmed” means only accounts at least 30 days old with 500 edits can edit; “full” means only administrators.
Hidden note
A message in an article's source text that readers do not see, left for other editors.
Work
One book, article or web page listed in a footnote. A footnote can list several.

How labels are placed from left to right

A source is placed on the left–right line by its recorded labels. Labels that are positions (far-left, left-wing, centre-left, centrist, centre-right, right-wing, far-right) are used as they are. Only when a source has none of those, other labels are placed if English Wikipedia's article on that ideology (or on liberalism in the source's country, for "liberal") states one position for it as a whole; a label defined there as a variant of an ideology already placed takes that ideology's position. Where the article describes the ideology on more than one side, or does not say, the label is not placed. A label with two positions counts half for each; a source with several placed labels is split equally. "Liberal" is placed only for a source with exactly one recorded country, and only for the countries listed. What is and isn't AI

The analysis has no AI in it: it only counts and matches, the same way every time. AI was used to build the project: the code and the first drafts of this site's text were written with Claude, an AI assistant, and then checked as described below. The checks are there so that you do not have to trust the code, or whoever wrote it.

How the work is checked

  • Totals add up. Every editor's share in an article sums to 100%: passed for 2054 of 2054 articles.
  • Reproducible. Running the analysis again on the same version gives byte-for-byte identical data: passed for 2054 of 2054 articles.
  • Sentences checked against the actual edits. For 20 randomly chosen sentences, the edit credited with writing each one was opened. The check confirms that edit really added those words, and that the account that saved it is the editor credited: 20 of 20 confirmed.
  • Every sanction verified. Each entry on the sanctions list was opened at its link and the quoted decision confirmed: 130 of 130 entries confirmed.
  • No usernames on the pages. Every page is scanned for every account name in the article's history: passed for 2040 of 2054 articles.

Rules the project follows

  • Editors are not named. Article pages show neutral labels (Editor 1, Editor 2) that differ from page to page. The page on editors across articles uses separate pattern IDs, and shows editors Wikipedia has not sanctioned only by topic group, not by article. Usernames appear only behind links to Wikipedia's own public pages, such as an edit or a decision. The site is not designed to identify anyone; the underlying edit histories are public on Wikipedia for anyone to analyse.
  • Nobody is identified. No attempt is made to link accounts to real people, and no information from outside Wikipedia is used about any editor.
  • Same rules for everyone. Every figure is computed identically for every editor and article. The code has no notion of sides in any dispute.
  • Evidence for every number. Each figure traces to specific versions, edits or decisions, and the full data is downloadable.
  • No single score. There is no overall bias rating. Breakdowns with evidence only.
  • Read-only. The project never edits Wikipedia.

Limits

  • The sanctions list is not complete. It covers decisions from 2023 on, and is fullest for the Arab–Israeli conflict area. A figure of 0% means we found no sanction, not that there was none. Article pages flag where this matters.
  • A sanction is about conduct, not content. It is a finding about how an editor behaved, not about the accuracy of any text they wrote.
  • Comparisons are between analysed articles. A “typical” figure is the median of the other analysed articles of the same kind, which are the most-read ones, not a sample of everything Wikipedia has on the subject.
  • A snapshot. Each page describes one version. Wikipedia keeps changing, and the date is shown on every page.
