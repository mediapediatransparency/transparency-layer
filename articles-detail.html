Which articles, and why

Articles are chosen by a fixed rule, not by hand, so the selection does not depend on what anyone expects to find.

The rule

In short: How we choose which articles to analyse.

  1. Contentious topic areas. Wikipedia itself marks articles in subject areas where editing is under special rules (for example the Arab–Israeli conflict, US politics, gender and sexuality, Eastern Europe). It does this with a notice on each article's talk page (the discussion page behind every article). Every article with such a notice is a candidate.
  2. Kind of subject. Each candidate is classed as a journalist or commentator, another public figure, a news outlet, an organisation, an idea or movement, or an event, using Wikidata (Wikipedia's sister database). Articles that fit none of these, and “List of…” articles, are left out.
  3. Most read. In each area, for each kind, we take the 25 articles that were read most often over the previous 60 days.
  4. On request. A few articles are added on request. They are marked as such.

Only an article's own talk page counts. Notices and banners are sometimes placed on the talk page of a redirect (a page that only forwards readers to an article, such as a misspelling or an old title). Such a tag is not counted for the article it forwards to, because nobody placed it on that article. This rule was added on 5 November 2026; the Categories page lists the articles it affects and the redirects that carry the tags.

The lists are drawn again from time to time, with fresh page views, so an article can enter or leave a list as readership changes. An article analysed earlier keeps its page on this site after it leaves a list.

Kept articles. When a redrawn list no longer includes an article that has already been analysed, it stays on the list, marked “kept” in the Rank column. The reason is only technical: the analysis was already done, so keeping it costs nothing. It says nothing about the article. Most kept articles dropped out on 11 November 2026, when new kinds of events and ideas were added (genocides, famines, mass shootings, assassinations, pogroms, riots, genocide denial) and pushed less-read articles below the top 25. An article that was never analysed is simply dropped.

A second rule, for subjects Wikipedia does not tag

Wikipedia does not mark some subjects as contentious, or marks only a small part of them. These are: China (beyond the Uyghur and Xinjiang area), socialism, Islam, conservatism and the right, discrimination (including antisemitism and the far right), and libertarianism (which on Wikipedia also covers liberalism).

For these, we start from the articles that a WikiProject has tagged as its own. A WikiProject is a group of Wikipedia editors who look after one subject, and they decide which articles to tag. Steps 2 and 3 above are then the same. These areas are marked “chosen by subject”, because here we chose the subjects, not Wikipedia.

  • China, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Tibet: WikiProject China, WikiProject Taiwan, WikiProject Hong Kong, WikiProject Tibet
  • Socialism, communism and Marxism: WikiProject Socialism
  • Islam: WikiProject Islam
  • Conservatism and the right: WikiProject Conservatism
  • Discrimination, including antisemitism and the far right: WikiProject Discrimination
  • Libertarianism, which on Wikipedia also covers liberalism: WikiProject Libertarianism
About the classes: Wikidata's classification is used as it stands, with two rules added on 5 November 2026: movements that Wikidata also records as organisations are placed with ideas and movements unless Wikidata gives them a headquarters, chairperson or member count; and a journalist must be described as one in Wikidata, not only have journalism among their occupations. The Categories page lists the articles each rule moves. An article can appear in more than one area; it is analysed once and listed in each.
